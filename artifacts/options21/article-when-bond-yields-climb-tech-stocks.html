When Bond Yields Climb: What It Means for Tech Stocks

How yields, inflation, central banks and energy fit together, and where tech shares sit in the chain.

👤
Paul Wise
Options21 · AFSL 247412
Published2 Oct 2026
Read time6 min
CategoryMacro & Geopolitics
The short version
US bond yields have jumped in two months, and the 10-year is now at its highest level since 2007. The surprise is why: inflation has not been getting worse. The rise is coming from the real return on bonds, driven by heavy government and corporate borrowing, higher energy costs and a Fed that has started hiking again. When bonds pay this much, expensive growth shares, tech in particular, have less room for error.

How they connect

Energy pushes prices up. Oil, diesel, gas and electricity all rose over the summer. US petrol prices are up 27% on a year ago.

Central banks respond. The Fed raised rates on 16 September, its first hike since 2023. The ECB, Bank of Japan and RBA have all lifted rates this year as well.

Bond yields rise. But look closer: the market's inflation expectation is steady at about 2.35%. The jump is in the real yield (2.93%), the extra return investors now demand for lending to the US government.

Shares are re-priced. A share is worth the profits it will earn in future, discounted back to today. The higher the discount rate, the less distant profits are worth.

What it means for tech stocks

Tech is the most sensitive. Much of a tech company's value sits in profits years away, so a higher discount rate hits it hardest.

Shares are paying no extra for the risk. Expected earnings yield on the S&P 500 is about 5.2% (forward P/E 19.2), against 5.29% on the 10-year bond. The usual cushion for owning shares over bonds is close to zero.

The market is narrowing. The Nasdaq rose 1.9% in September, but chip stocks (SOX) fell 11% over the quarter, the Russell 2000 is more than 8% below its August peak, and only 24% of US stocks are above their 50-day average (over 70% in mid-August). A few large names are carrying the index.

AI spending is now funded with debt. Big tech has issued about US$220bn of bonds this year to August, against US$121bn in all of 2025. These bonds pay about 1.15% over Treasuries versus 0.78% for typical investment-grade issuers, and demand for them has cooled. That competes with the Treasury for the same buyers.

Can the US afford its debt?

Total US debt passed US$40 trillion in August. The US cannot run out of US dollars, so default is not the issue. The issue is the cost. Interest already costs about US$1 trillion a year, more than the defence budget, or roughly one dollar in five of tax revenue. The government is paying an average 3.5% on its debt today, while new debt costs about 5%. As old, cheaper debt matures and is replaced, the bill rises. Illustratively, if all marketable debt repriced to 5%, interest would be about US$485bn a year higher. The next flashpoint is the debt limit, expected to bite around spring 2027.

What to watch

  • The 10-year yield holding above 5% and the real yield near 3%.
  • The Fed meeting on 27–28 October (markets see about a 35–38% chance of another hike).
  • Chip earnings, starting with Micron.
  • Energy supply through the Strait of Hormuz and Europe's low gas storage.
  • Falling bond demand at Treasury and big-tech auctions.

Rising yields are usually blamed on inflation. Here the evidence points elsewhere: investors want a higher real return to lend, at a time when government and big-tech borrowing is heavy. That is a harder problem for expensive shares than a temporary inflation scare.
