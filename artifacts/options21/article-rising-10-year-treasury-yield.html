Why the 10-Year Treasury Yield Keeps Rising

Inflation, energy prices, economic growth, government borrowing and AI investment are all adding pressure to long-term yields. Here is how that can flow through to technology shares, bonds and Australian portfolios.

Published24 Sep 2026
Read time16 min
CategoryMacro & Geopolitics
The trade conclusion
The 10-year yield is rising for several reasons at once—not because of one auction or one Fed decision. My view is cautious, not strongly bearish: if the yield stays near or above 5%, share valuations may face more pressure, but higher yields alone do not guarantee a market crash. The bigger risk is rising yields alongside falling earnings forecasts.

The investor answer

Common questions about the rising 10-year yield
QuestionRetail investor answer
Why is the 10-year rising?Inflation, energy prices, growth, government borrowing, AI spending and the extra return investors want for holding long-term bonds.
Why does the 10-year matter?It helps set mortgage rates, company borrowing costs, share values and many global prices.
Why is technology more sensitive?More of its expected profits are far in the future, so higher rates reduce their value more.
What would reverse the pressure?Lower inflation, lower energy prices, slower growth, lower expected rates or a flight to safety.

Scope and warning: This note uses US data because the US 10-year Treasury is a key global interest rate. Australian investors also face AUD/USD currency risk and the effect of global rates on Australian bonds and shares. This is general information, not personal advice.

What moves the 10-year yield?

A simple way to think about it

10-year yield = expected average short-term interest rates + expected inflation + term premium

The first part is what investors expect short-term rates to average over the next ten years. The second is protection against inflation. The term premium is the extra return investors want for lending money for ten years while facing interest-rate, inflation, government-debt and market risks.

Factors contributing to the 10-year Treasury yield
DriverWhat has been happeningWhy it can push the yield higher
Expected short-term ratesThe Fed raised rates to 3.75%–4.00% in September. Markets still see a risk of more increases.[1]Investors want more yield if short-term rates may stay high for longer.
Inflation compensationPCE inflation was 4.1% in May and core PCE was 3.4%, both above the Fed’s 2% goal.[2]Investors want compensation for the loss of purchasing power.
Term premiumLarge deficits, heavy long-term borrowing and uncertain inflation can make investors demand more return.[3][4]Long-term bond prices can still fall even if expected Fed rates stop rising.
Growth and capital demandThe Fed says growth, productivity and investment remain strong.[1]A strong economy reduces demand for safe-haven bonds and can push real yields higher.

The 2-year and 10-year are different

The 2-year Treasury mainly reflects expected Fed policy over the next few years. The 10-year also reflects long-term inflation, economic growth, government borrowing and demand from large investors. That is why the 10-year can rise even when investors expect the Fed to cut short-term rates later.

Why 5% matters—but is not a magic line

The 10-year briefly tested 5% in September. Some investors see 5% as a warning level for shares, but there is no rule that stocks must fall at exactly 5%. The effect depends on why yields are rising, how quickly they rise, valuations, earnings and credit conditions.[5][6]

Inflation and rising energy prices

Why energy matters

Energy affects bonds in two ways. Higher oil, gas and electricity prices push inflation higher. They also raise transport, manufacturing, food and data-centre costs. If households and businesses expect prices to keep rising, the inflation problem can spread.

An energy shock can also leave households with less money to spend and cut company margins. This can create stagflation—higher inflation and weaker growth. That is difficult for both bonds and shares because the central bank cannot easily cut rates without risking more inflation.

The Fed reported that energy prices in its PCE measure rose 24% in the year to May. It also reported total inflation of 4.1% and core inflation of 3.4%. The Middle East conflict and disrupted shipping helped push energy prices higher.[2]

Energy prices can fall again

Oil prices can fall if shipping improves, supply increases or demand weakens. A one-off price rise should eventually drop out of annual inflation. The risk is greater if high energy prices start to affect wages, rents, services and inflation expectations.

Government borrowing competes for money

Treasury borrowing is large

The Treasury expected to borrow $739 billion from private investors in July–September 2026 and $628 billion in October–December, subject to cash assumptions.[7] These are borrowing estimates, not yield forecasts. Heavy and persistent borrowing can still force the government to offer higher yields, especially when inflation is high.

SIFMA reported $31.1 trillion of US Treasury securities outstanding in Q2 2026.[8] The government can find buyers. The issue is how much yield buyers demand when the Treasury and large companies are both issuing long-term debt.

AI adds another large borrower

The Dallas Fed says AI data-centre finance can add long-term risk to bond markets through corporate bonds, private loans and interest-rate swaps. Its example put possible AI-related supply at up to $360 billion of 10-year-equivalent exposure in 2026. This was an estimate—not a statement that all of it had already been issued.[9]

Technology bonds do not replace Treasuries as the main safe asset. But investors, banks and insurers have limits on how much long-term risk they can hold. More long-term corporate debt can therefore push required yields higher.

Crowding out means higher prices for money

Higher yields can attract more savings and overseas money. Companies can also raise shares, use cash, slow spending or ask customers to pay in advance. AI borrowing does not prove that other businesses cannot borrow. It is more accurate to say that borrowing may become more expensive, especially for weaker companies.

Why technology shares feel the pressure

Why rates affect share prices

A share price reflects the value of future cash flows. When interest rates rise, cash expected in ten years becomes worth less today than cash expected next year. The 10-year Treasury is an important starting point for this calculation.

For example, a $100 cash flow received in ten years is worth about $61 when discounted at 5%. At 6%, it is worth about $56. That is an 8% fall before the company’s business changes. The further away the cash flow, the greater the effect.

Why growth shares are more exposed

Ways higher 10-year yields can affect growth shares
Transmission channelWhat can happen when the 10-year yield rises
Valuation multipleThe present value of distant earnings falls; price-to-earnings and price-to-sales multiples can compress.
Relative appealA 5% government yield competes more directly with equities that offer uncertain future cash flows.
Financing costsDebt, leases and project finance become more expensive; interest expense rises.
Investment hurdle rateProjects must earn more to justify investment, potentially slowing marginal AI and cloud projects.
Currency and global liquidityHigher US yields can support the dollar and tighten global financial conditions, affecting emerging markets and commodities.
Market concentrationIf a few mega-cap technology stocks carry a large share of index returns, their repricing can affect the entire index.

Higher yields do not always mean lower technology profits

Higher yields can also mean the economy is growing strongly. A company with rising sales, pricing power and strong cash flow may still perform well. AI productivity gains could support profits. The greatest risk is when heavy spending is funded with debt but the promised revenue arrives late or disappoints.

The most vulnerable companies

The riskiest company is highly valued, has weak current cash flow, needs regular financing, depends on a few customers and is priced for near-perfect results. A profitable company can still fall in price, but strong cash reserves reduce the risk of lasting damage.

What to monitor in shares

Markets can handle higher yields if earnings forecasts rise even faster. The warning sign is different: yields rise, earnings forecasts fall, credit spreads widen and fewer shares take part in the rally. That combination matters more than the 10-year level by itself.

A simple plan for investors

Base case: more volatility

While inflation, energy prices and government borrowing remain high, long-term bonds and expensive growth shares may stay volatile. This does not mean selling every technology share or shorting Treasuries. It means not relying on rates to fall forever or share valuations to keep expanding.

Questions to consider when reviewing portfolio exposure
Investor concernPractical response to consider
I need income and cannot tolerate large price swingsPrefer shorter-duration, high-quality bonds or staggered maturities; accept reinvestment risk if rates fall.
I want recession protectionUse high-quality government bonds, but match duration to your ability to tolerate interim losses.
I own expensive growth sharesStress-test the valuation using a higher discount rate and lower terminal growth; check free cash flow and debt maturities.
I want AI exposurePrefer businesses with cash generation, pricing power and funding flexibility over highly leveraged project operators.
I hold US assets from AustraliaSeparate asset-market risk from AUD/USD risk; decide whether currency exposure is intentional.
I am tempted to trade the 5% levelUse it as a risk marker, not a mechanical buy or sell signal.

A simple stress test

For a bond fund with a duration of 7 years, a 1 percentage-point rise in yields could reduce its price by about 7%, before income. For a duration of 2 years, the effect is about 2%. Long-duration funds can gain more when yields fall, but they can also lose more when yields rise.

For shares, test what happens if the 10-year yield is 0.50 to 1.00 percentage point higher. Then test slower sales growth, lower profit margins and a higher risk premium. If the investment case fails after small changes, it depends on very optimistic assumptions.

For Australian investors

A US bond or share investment has two moving parts: the asset price and the USD/AUD exchange rate. A higher US yield may support the US dollar, but this can reverse during a market panic. Hedging can reduce currency swings but adds costs. Check duration, credit quality, concentration, fees and currency hedging before treating a bond fund as defensive.

Key takeaways
  • The 10-year yield reflects expected short-term rates, inflation compensation and the term premium; it can rise even when investors expect future Fed cuts.
  • Persistent energy inflation, heavy borrowing and greater long-term debt supply can add pressure, while stronger growth may also push real yields higher.
  • Higher yields can compress valuations, especially for growth shares whose expected cash flows are further in the future. But they do not automatically trigger a market crash.
  • The more concerning combination is rising yields alongside falling earnings forecasts, wider credit spreads, higher energy costs and weakening market breadth.
  • Treat 5% as a risk marker, not a mechanical buy or sell signal; stress-test both duration and equity assumptions.
  • Australian investors in US assets should consider both market exposure and AUD/USD currency risk.

Important limits

Many forces move the 10-year yield. No source can say exactly how many basis points came from energy, government borrowing, AI spending or Fed policy. The evidence points to more risk for long-term bonds and expensive growth shares, not a guaranteed market crash. The key question is what happens to earnings: rising yields with rising earnings may be manageable; rising yields with falling earnings, wider credit spreads and higher energy costs are much more dangerous.
