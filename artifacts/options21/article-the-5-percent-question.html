The 5% Question: Why a Rate-Hiking Fed and a $2 Trillion Deficit Matter for Your Portfolio

For most of the past 15 years, investors could lean on one comforting assumption: when things got rough, the US Federal Reserve would eventually cut rates. That assumption is being tested.

The short version
For most of the past 15 years, investors could lean on one comforting assumption: when things got rough, the US Federal Reserve would eventually cut rates. That assumption is being tested. This week a hotter-than-expected US inflation report pushed the 10-year Treasury yield back to around 5% and left traders pricing a December rate hike, possibly a big one [2][3]. Because the 10-year yield is the world's benchmark 'risk-free' rate, where it settles over the next few years could shape returns on almost everything, from tech shares and gold to bitcoin and the Aussie dollar.

A hot inflation print and a Fed in no mood to wait

US consumer prices rose 0.4% in October against 0.3% expected, while core prices (excluding food and energy) rose 0.3% against 0.2% expected [2], the third straight upside surprise [4]. Fed Governor Christopher Waller said that if inflation ran hot, a 50 basis point hike should be considered in December, adding that “hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk” [2]. The two-year yield jumped 17 basis points to 4.73%, its biggest one-day rise since the April 2025 tariff shock [1].

Context matters here. The Fed has raised rates once (a quarter point in September) since Kevin Warsh became chair this year, and Warsh has dropped the habit of giving forward guidance, so markets are reacting sharply to each data point and each Fed speech [1]. Futures now imply roughly three quarter-point hikes by June [1], and CME FedWatch showed an 11% chance of a half-point move in December, up from zero a week earlier [3].

Why long-term yields are stuck high: the debt bill

The pressure is not only about the Fed. In October, 10-year and 30-year yields hit 24-year highs [2]. The US ran a $1.992 trillion deficit in fiscal 2026, about 6.2% of GDP [5], and paid $1.052 trillion in net interest, which is more than it spent on defence and an estimated record 3.3% of GDP [6]. The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) assumes the 10-year yield averages 4.3% over the next decade; if rates instead run half a point higher, it estimates ten-year deficits $1.9 trillion larger, and a 1.5-point overshoot would add $6 trillion [5].

That creates a feedback loop: higher yields mean bigger deficits, more bond issuance and investors demanding more compensation to hold long-dated debt. With the midterms delivering divided government, meaningful deficit repair looks unlikely for at least two years [5]. Demand is already showing strain. This week's 10-year auction sold at 5.02%, above where the notes had been trading [1], and dealers were left holding 10% of it, the most since May [2].

AI’s thirst for capital adds to the squeeze

A third force is the artificial intelligence build-out. T. Rowe Price estimates that consensus 2027 capital spending across the largest tech companies has risen 131% since the end of 2025 to close to US$1.5 trillion, while expected free cash flow has swung from about US$165 billion to roughly negative US$237 billion, so these companies are increasingly borrowing [7]. Its base case is that around US$1.8 trillion of compute-backed credit could be raised between 2026 and 2030. In its view, this competition for savings is helping push real interest rates higher; US 10-year real yields rose nearly a full percentage point between April and September [7]. In short, governments and tech giants are bidding for the same pool of capital.

What it could mean for markets

Bonds: Long-duration bond ETFs are the most sensitive to further rises in yields, while short-dated bonds and cash track the Fed's path more closely. On the plus side, yields near 5% offer a much bigger income buffer than in years past.

Equities: Higher discount rates weigh most on richly valued growth stocks. On the CPI day the Nasdaq 100 fell 0.6% and Nvidia 5.5%, while financials and energy held up better [4]. Rate-sensitive housing is already softening, with US mortgage rates around 7.5% and pending home sales down 4% in September [5].

Currencies: The US dollar bounced on the yield jump, and AUD/USD tested its lowest levels since early this year, below 0.6850 [4], a reminder that Fed surprises flow quickly into the Aussie dollar.

Gold and crypto: Gold slid about 2% to near US$4,000 after the CPI data before rebounding to around US$4,055 [4]. Bitcoin sat near an 11-week low around US$73,700 as spot bitcoin ETFs recorded a fourth straight day of outflows [4].

Credit and AI infrastructure: Higher yields hit debt-funded data-centre builders [4], yet US high-yield spreads were a tight 320 basis points [4], leaving little cushion if conditions sour. T. Rowe Price flags weak interest coverage as the first sign of stress [7].

Risks and counterpoints

This is not a one-way story. Vanguard's Josh Hirt argues that beneath recent outsized shocks, the US economy is behaving more in line with the Fed's goals, so modest policy moves may be enough [3]. Oil is also easing: Brent briefly dipped below US$90 on hopes the US-Iran war could end soon, which would take some heat out of inflation [2]. T. Rowe Price's downside scenario is an AI capacity glut, in which credit spreads widen and Treasuries rally rather than sell off [7]. And as CNBC notes, expensive borrowing is not the same as a crisis, as high yields also attract buyers to US bonds [5].

What to watch

The next signposts are October producer prices, upcoming Treasury auctions and the Fed's 8–9 December meeting [4].
