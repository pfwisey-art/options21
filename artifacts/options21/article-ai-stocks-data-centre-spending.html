AI Stocks and Data Centre Spending

Rising CDS costs may be a warning signal about the borrowing behind the AI data centre boom. This note explains what investors can learn from CoreWeave’s cash flow and what to watch next.

👤
Paul Wise
Options21 · AFSL 247412
Published22 Oct 2026
Read time3 min
CategoryAI & Technology
The short version
Rising CDS protection costs across several AI-related companies may signal debt investors’ growing concern about borrowing for data centre expansion. CoreWeave’s first-half 2026 operating cash flow was about US$3.7 billion against about US$14.1 billion in property and equipment spending. These figures illustrate funding needs; they do not establish an inability to repay debt or predict share prices.

Credit default swaps in plain English

A credit default swap, or CDS, works like insurance against a company failing to pay its debts. An investor pays for protection. If a covered default occurs, the protection seller pays compensation under the contract. [1]

For example, in a simplified contract, protecting US$100,000 of company debt at 2% costs US$2,000 a year. If a covered default leaves that debt worth US$40,000, the protection would pay US$60,000 before premiums and costs. Actual contracts can include upfront payments. [1]

When protection becomes more expensive, it can signal growing concern about a company’s ability to repay debt. Share investors can use this as a warning sign, although it does not tell them what the share price will do.

Why data centre spending matters

Data centres house the computers that train and run AI systems. Building them requires expensive chips, buildings, electricity and cooling. Some companies can pay from existing cash and profits. Others depend heavily on borrowing.

This spending creates sales for chipmakers and equipment suppliers. The companies paying for the data centres then need enough paying customers to cover their costs and earn a profit.

Problems can emerge if construction spending runs ahead of income, projects are delayed or borrowing becomes more expensive. A company can have strong sales growth while still needing more money to fund its expansion.

A warning signal for share markets

The rising cost of CDS protection across several AI-related companies suggests debt investors are growing more concerned about the borrowing behind the data centre boom. [3] For share markets, this can be a warning signal: tighter or more expensive funding could slow expansion, squeeze profits and reduce orders for chips and equipment. If those pressures spread, the effects could reach the wider share market. Rising CDS costs do not guarantee a market fall, but a sustained increase across several companies deserves attention.

CoreWeave as an example

CoreWeave (NASDAQ: CRWV) rents AI computing capacity. In the first half of 2026, operating cash flow was about US$3.7 billion, while cash spending on property and equipment was about US$14.1 billion. [2]

That gap illustrates how rapid expansion can depend on cash reserves and outside funding. It does not, by itself, establish that a company cannot pay its debts.

What retail investors should watch

  • Cash coming in compared with money spent on expansion.
  • Debt levels, interest bills and when loans must be repaid.
  • Whether new data centres are being completed, used and paid for.

The trade view

Stay selective across AI stocks. Companies with strong cash generation and manageable debt have more room to handle delays or weaker demand. Higher CDS costs warrant a closer look at a borrower’s finances. Growing data centre spending alone is not enough to make a share attractive.
